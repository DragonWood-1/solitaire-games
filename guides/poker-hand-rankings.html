Poker Hand Rankings Explained: Kickers, Outs and Pot Odds

Almost everyone who sits down at a poker table for the first time has already memorised the list of hands. Flush beats straight, full house beats flush, and so on down the line. What trips new players up is not the list itself but everything that surrounds it: what happens when two players hold the same category of hand, how five cards get chosen out of the seven available in Texas Hold'em, and how to decide whether a draw is worth paying to chase. This guide covers all three, in that order, because they build on one another.

The ten hand rankings, strongest to weakest

Every poker hand is exactly five cards. Categories are compared first; only if two hands fall into the same category do you compare the cards inside them. Suits never break ties in standard poker — a spade flush and a heart flush of identical ranks split the pot.

Standard poker hand rankings and their tie-breakers

5FlushA♦ J♦ 8♦ 5♦ 3♦Compare highest card, then second, then third, and so on down to the fifth. All five cards can act as kickers.
6Straight8♦ 7♠ 6♠ 5♥ 4♣Highest top card wins. No kicker. Identical straights split the pot, which happens often.
7Three of a kind5♠ 5♥ 5♦ A♣ 9♥Higher trips win. If equal, compare the highest kicker, then the second kicker.

Two details that catch people out

The ace is both high and low for straights. A-2-3-4-5, known as the wheel, is a legitimate straight, but it is the lowest straight — the ace plays as a one, so the wheel loses to 2-3-4-5-6. Straights do not wrap around: Q-K-A-2-3 is nothing at all.

A flush always beats a straight, and the reason is simply that flushes are rarer. There are 10,200 possible straights in a 52-card deck against 5,108 flushes. Rankings follow scarcity throughout the list, which is a useful sanity check if you ever forget the order.

Kickers: the part beginners underestimate

A kicker is any card in your five that is not part of the made combination, used to break ties. It is the single most common source of "but I had the same hand!" confusion.

Suppose the board is K♦ 9♣ 4♥ 2♠ 7♦. You hold A♠ K♣ and your opponent holds K♠ J♥. You both have a pair of kings. Your best five cards are K♦ K♣ A♠ 9♣ 7♦; theirs are K♦ K♠ J♥ 9♣ 7♦. Ace beats jack, so you win with the better kicker. Nothing about the pair changed — the fight was decided by a card that was not part of the pair at all.

This is why hands like K-7 and Q-6 are dangerous. They make top pair often enough to feel good, and then lose to A-K, K-Q and K-J when the money goes in. Experienced players call this being dominated: your hand shares a card with your opponent's and is behind on the other one, so improvement rarely helps.

Kickers stop mattering as soon as all five of your cards are locked in. Straights and flushes that use the board entirely, or hands where the board itself is the best five cards, produce split pots. If the board reads 9-T-J-Q-K and neither player holds an ace, everyone plays the board and the pot is chopped.

Making five from seven in Hold'em

In Texas Hold'em you receive two private cards and share five community cards. Your hand is the best possible five-card combination from those seven, and — this is the part people misstate — you may use both hole cards, one, or none. There is no requirement to use your own cards at all.

The mechanical way to do it, when you are learning, is to check the seven cards for each category from the top down. Are there five of one suit? Five in sequence? Any triple, and if so does a pair accompany it? Then pairs. Take the first category you find, then fill any remaining slots with the highest unused cards as kickers.

Worked example. You hold A♠ 5♠. The board is K♠ 9♠ 5♥ 2♠ J♦. Your seven cards contain four spades plus your A♠ — that is A♠ K♠ 9♠ 5♠ 2♠, an ace-high flush. Note that your pair of fives is irrelevant; the flush is a stronger category and consumes all five slots. Note also that if a fifth board spade had appeared, everyone would have a flush and your A♠ would still be the deciding card, because your ace outranks any spade on the board.

A second example, this time a trap. You hold 7♥ 7♦ and the board is 7♠ K♣ K♦ Q♠ Q♥. It is tempting to see three sevens and feel strong. Your best five are actually 7♠ 7♥ 7♦ K♣ K♦ — a full house, sevens full of kings. But an opponent holding any king or queen has a bigger full house, and the two pair on the board means even a lone king beats you. Reading the board as a whole, rather than looking only at your own cards, is a skill worth practising deliberately.

Outs and pot odds, with the arithmetic spelled out

An out is a card still in the deck that turns a losing hand into a winning one. Counting outs is the foundation of every drawing decision.

Say you hold J♥ T♥ and the flop is A♥ 7♥ 3♣. Any heart completes a flush. There are thirteen hearts; you can see four of them, so nine remain. You have nine outs. If you also decided a jack or ten might win, you could add those, but they are weaker outs — the ace on board means a pair of jacks is often behind. Honest out-counting means discounting cards that improve you into second-best hands.

Pot odds ask whether the price you are being offered justifies the chase. The pot holds $80 and your opponent bets $20, making it $100. You must call $20 to win $100, so you are getting 5-to-1, which is to say you need to win at least 1 time in 6 — about 16.7% — to break even.

Now the equity side. With nine outs and one card to come, 47 cards are unseen (52 minus your 2 minus the board's 3). Nine of them help: 9 ÷ 47 = 19.1%. Since 19.1% is greater than the 16.7% you need, calling is profitable. Turn the numbers around — if the bet were $60 into $80, you would be calling $60 to win $140, needing 30%, and the same draw becomes a fold.

The rule of 2 and 4

Nobody wants to do division at the table, so use this shortcut. Multiply your outs by 2 for the chance of hitting on the next card. Multiply by 4 for the chance of hitting across both the turn and the river, but only when the remaining money is going in now — otherwise you will face another bet on the turn and the 4 overstates your position.

Nine outs × 2 = 18%, against the true 19.1%. Nine outs × 4 = 36%, against a true figure near 35%. Both are close enough to make decisions with. The approximation drifts high with large out counts: fifteen outs gives 60% by the rule, where the real number is roughly 54%, so shade big draws downward a little in your head.

Position

Position means how late you act in a betting round. Acting last is a structural advantage that never goes away, because you make every decision knowing what everyone else did first. The same hand is worth meaningfully more in late position than in early position, which is why opening ranges tighten as you move toward the blinds and widen on the button.

Practically, being in position lets you take free cards with a draw by checking behind, extract an extra bet when opponents show weakness, and fold cheaply when they show strength. Out of position you are guessing, and guessing costs money over thousands of hands. If you make one adjustment after reading this page, make it this: play fewer marginal hands from early seats.

Common beginner errors

  • Playing too many starting hands. Suited cards look pretty but only make a flush about 6.5% of the time by the river. Suitedness adds a few points of equity; it does not rescue weak ranks.
  • Calling to "see what happens." Every call needs a reason: pot odds on a draw, or a belief that your made hand is best. Curiosity is the most expensive motive in poker.
  • Overvaluing top pair with a weak kicker. Ace-rag is the classic bankroll leak. When the big money arrives, someone usually has the better kicker.
  • Ignoring the board's texture. Three cards of one suit, or three connected cards, change the value of every hand. A set on a coordinated board is far less safe than a set on a rainbow of unconnected ranks.
  • Chasing without counting. Draws are not automatically worth continuing. Count the outs, compare with the price, and be willing to fold a flush draw to a large bet.
  • Bet sizing that telegraphs. Betting large with strong hands and small with weak ones is readable within an hour. Size to the pot and to what you want called, not to how excited you feel.
  • Playing tilted. A bad beat does not change the odds of the next hand. Stand up for ten minutes instead.

How to practise this

Read the rankings table until you can recite the tie-break rule for each category without looking, then move to board reading: deal seven cards face up, name the best five, and say what beats them. After that, drill out counting — flop a random hand, count the outs honestly, convert with the rule of 2 and 4, and only then decide. Doing these three drills for twenty minutes at a time is worth more than hours of casual play, because each repetition targets exactly the skill that decides real hands.
