Chess for Beginners: From Piece Movement to Your First Checkmate

Almost everybody learns chess twice. The first time, somebody shows you which way the pieces go, you play a few games, you lose them in confusing ways, and you conclude that chess is a memory contest for people with unusual brains. The second time — if there is a second time — somebody explains why the moves matter, and the game turns into something you can actually reason about. This guide is written to be that second lesson, compressed into one sitting. It assumes you know nothing, and it tries never to tell you a rule without telling you what the rule is for.

Chess is played on an eight-by-eight board of alternating light and dark squares, oriented so that each player has a light square in their bottom-right corner. White moves first, then players alternate, and no move may ever be skipped. The single goal is to trap the enemy king so completely that it cannot escape capture. Everything else in the game — every piece, every principle, every tactic below — exists in service of that one objective, or of preventing your opponent from achieving it first.

How Each Piece Moves

The Pawn

Pawns are the most rule-heavy pieces on the board, which is a strange thing to say about the ones beginners take least seriously. A pawn moves straight forward one square, but never backward — pawns are the only pieces in chess that cannot retreat, which means every pawn move is a permanent commitment. On its very first move, a pawn has the option of advancing two squares instead of one. Critically, a pawn captures differently from how it moves: it takes diagonally forward, one square to either side. A pawn facing an enemy piece directly in front of it is stuck; it cannot capture what it is blocked by.

The Knight

The knight moves in an L: two squares in one direction, then one square perpendicular. It is the only piece that jumps — pieces in between are simply irrelevant to it, which makes knights dangerous in crowded positions where everything else is bottled up. A knight on a central square controls up to eight squares; a knight in the corner controls two. This is the origin of the old advice "a knight on the rim is dim." Knights also always change square colour with each move, a quirk that matters more than you would expect in endgames.

The Bishop

The bishop slides any number of unobstructed squares diagonally. Because a diagonal never changes colour, each bishop is permanently confined to the light squares or the dark squares it started on. You begin with one of each, and the pair working together covers the whole board — which is why trading off one bishop and keeping the other often leaves lasting weaknesses on the colour you abandoned.

The Rook

The rook slides any number of unobstructed squares horizontally or vertically. Rooks are clumsy early, when the board is cluttered, and brutal later, when files have opened. A rook controls fourteen squares from anywhere on an empty board, which makes it the most consistent long-range piece in the game.

The Queen

The queen combines rook and bishop: any distance, any of the eight directions, provided the path is clear. She is overwhelmingly the strongest piece, and beginners routinely ruin games by bringing her out early to attack things. A queen is worth so much that any lesser piece attacking her forces her to run, so an exposed queen becomes a target that develops your opponent's army for them.

The King

The king moves exactly one square in any direction. He may never move onto a square attacked by an enemy piece, and the two kings can never stand adjacent. In the opening and middlegame the king is a liability to be hidden; in the endgame, once the queens are gone, he becomes a genuinely strong attacking piece — roughly as valuable as a minor piece in fighting strength. Learning to march the king forward in the endgame is one of the clearest markers of a player who has moved past beginner level.

Relative Piece Values

Because you constantly face the question "is this trade good?", players use an approximate point system. These numbers are a rule of thumb, not a law — position frequently overrides them — but they are the right default when you have nothing better to go on.

PieceApproximate valuePractical note
Pawn1The unit of measurement; can promote to a queen
Knight3Best in closed, blocked positions
Bishop3Slightly better than a knight in open positions
Rook5Needs open files to matter
Queen9Strongest piece; do not expose it early
King—Infinite: losing it loses the game

Two useful consequences: trading a rook for a knight or bishop loses roughly two points and is called "losing the exchange," and three minor pieces are usually worth more than a queen despite the equal-looking arithmetic, because three pieces can attack three places at once.

Check, Checkmate and Stalemate

When a piece attacks the enemy king, that is check. You are obliged to respond immediately, and there are exactly three legal responses: move the king to a safe square, capture the checking piece, or block the line between the checking piece and the king. Blocking is impossible against a knight or a pawn, since neither attacks along a line.

If you are in check and none of those three responses is available, that is checkmate, and the game ends immediately. The king is never actually captured; the game stops the moment capture becomes unavoidable.

Stalemate is the rule that costs beginners more won games than any other. If the player to move is not in check but has no legal move at all, the game is an immediate draw. This is why, when you are winning overwhelmingly, you must be careful about taking away every square from a bare king — a completely paralysed but unchecked king means you have thrown away the full point. The mirror image is also true: when you are hopelessly lost, hunting for stalemate is a real practical resource.

The Three Special Moves

Castling

Castling is the only move that shifts two pieces at once. The king travels two squares toward a rook, and that rook jumps over to the square the king passed. Kingside castling is short and fast; queenside castling is longer and usually leaves the king slightly more exposed. Castling is legal only when all of the following hold: the king has never moved, that rook has never moved, all squares between them are empty, the king is not currently in check, and the king does not pass through or land on an attacked square. Note the asymmetry — the rook may pass over an attacked square, but the king may not.

En Passant

En passant ("in passing") exists to close a loophole created by the two-square pawn advance. If your opponent pushes a pawn two squares and it lands directly beside one of your pawns, you may capture it on the very next move exactly as though it had only moved one square — your pawn moves diagonally to the square it skipped, and the enemy pawn is removed. The offer expires instantly: if you play anything else, the right is gone forever.

Pawn Promotion

A pawn that reaches the far end of the board is immediately replaced by a queen, rook, bishop or knight of its own colour. You are not limited to pieces already captured — nine queens on the board is legal, if absurd. Promoting to a queen is right the overwhelming majority of the time. The main exception is the knight, which occasionally delivers a check or fork that a queen simply cannot; the rook is chosen rarely, but exactly when a new queen would produce stalemate.

The Three Opening Principles

You do not need memorised opening lines to play a decent first ten moves. You need three ideas.

  1. Control the centre. The four central squares are the crossroads of the board. Pieces stationed there reach more squares and can swing to either wing quickly. Opening with a central pawn two squares forward stakes an immediate claim and simultaneously opens diagonals for your bishop and queen.
  2. Develop your pieces. Development means moving pieces off the back rank to squares where they do something. Bring out knights and bishops before rooks and queen, aim them toward the centre, and — this is the part beginners skip — try to move each piece only once in the opening. Every repeated move with the same piece is a move your opponent spends developing a new one.
  3. Get your king safe. In practice this means castling, usually within the first ten moves. A castled king sits behind an intact wall of pawns and away from the open centre where the fighting happens, and castling connects your rooks so they can defend each other.

Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Bringing the queen out on move two or three. Early queen raids either get refuted by simple development or waste four moves retreating.
  • Moving too many pawns. Pawn moves feel safe because nothing can be lost immediately, but they do not develop anything and they create permanent holes.
  • Grabbing material without checking what it costs. Before every capture, ask what recaptures. Free pawns are often bait.
  • Playing only your own plan. After every single opponent move, ask one question: what does that move attack? Most beginner losses are not deep strategic failures; they are pieces hanging in plain sight.
  • Ignoring the back rank. A castled king behind three untouched pawns can be checkmated by a single rook arriving on the last rank. Making a small escape square — pushing one pawn a square — costs one tempo and prevents an entire class of disaster.
  • Panicking when checked. Beginners reflexively move the king. Capturing or blocking is often far better, and moving the king forfeits castling rights permanently.

Basic Tactics

Tactics are short forcing sequences that win material or deliver mate. The great majority of games between beginners and improving players are decided by these four patterns, so recognising them is the single highest-return skill in chess.

The Fork

A fork is one piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at once. The defender can save only one. Knights are the classic forking piece, because their odd movement means a forked queen and rook cannot simply capture the attacker, but every piece can fork — pawns fork two pieces standing on adjacent diagonals, and a queen forks constantly. The most valuable fork of all is one where a piece is attacked and the king is in check, since the check must be answered first.

The Pin

A pin occurs when a piece cannot move because doing so would expose something more valuable behind it on the same line. Only the long-range sliders — bishop, rook, queen — can pin. When the piece behind is the king, the pin is absolute: moving the pinned piece is flatly illegal. When it is merely a valuable piece such as the queen, the pin is relative: moving is legal but costly. Pinned pieces are wonderful targets because they cannot run, so the standard follow-up is to pile more attackers onto the pinned piece, often a pawn.

The Skewer

A skewer is a pin turned inside out: the valuable piece is in front, and when it moves out of the attack, the lesser piece behind it is captured. A rook giving check along a rank to a king that has a rook sitting behind it is the archetype — the king must move, and the rook falls.

The Discovered Attack

A discovered attack happens when moving one piece unveils an attack from a piece behind it. Because the moving piece is free to do something aggressive of its own, discovered attacks generate two threats from a single move. The devastating version is the discovered check, where the unveiled line checks the king: the opponent must deal with the check, and the piece that moved away is free to capture almost anything it likes. Double check — where the moving piece and the unveiled piece both give check — cannot be met by capturing or blocking at all, so the king is legally forced to move.

Simple Endgame Technique

Endgames feel dry until you realise that knowing two or three of them converts every winning position into an actual win. Start with these.

King and Queen versus King

The method is to shrink the enemy king's living space rather than to chase it. Use the queen a knight's-move away from the lone king; from that distance she covers the escape squares without ever standing adjacent, and the defending king is confined to a shrinking box. Walk the box to the edge of the board, then bring your own king up to support, and only deliver mate once your king is close enough to cover the final flight squares. The single warning is stalemate: if the enemy king is in a corner and you take away every square without giving check, the game is drawn.

King and Rook versus King

The rook cannot do this alone; the two kings must cooperate. The standard technique is the "staircase": place the rook so it cuts the enemy king off along a rank or file, restricting it to part of the board. March your own king up until the two kings stand directly opposite each other with one square between them. Now check with the rook — the enemy king must retreat a rank, and you shift the cutting line one rank closer. Repeat until the defender is on the edge, then deliver mate along the final rank.

The Opposition

Opposition is the key concept in king-and-pawn endings. When the two kings face each other on the same file or rank with exactly one square between them, the player who does not have to move holds the opposition, and the one who must move is forced to give ground. Because kings can never be adjacent, the moving king must step sideways or backward and let the other one advance. Winning a pawn endgame usually comes down to reaching a position where you take the opposition and your opponent has to step aside, letting your king escort the pawn to promotion. Practise it with a single pawn and two kings; it is a small, sharp skill and it decides real games.

Where to Go From Here

The fastest way to improve from this point is not to study openings. It is to play full games at a slow enough time control that you can actually think, and then do two things every game: before each move, check what your opponent's last move threatens, and after each game, find the exact move where the game turned. Tactics come from pattern recognition, and pattern recognition comes from volume — a few tactical puzzles a day will teach your eye to spot forks and pins faster than any amount of reading. The rules above are complete enough to play the game properly. Everything else is practice.
