About this crossword

A crossword is a grid of white and black squares in which every white square belongs to at least one word, read either left to right (Across) or top to bottom (Down). Each numbered square starts a word, and each numbered word has a clue. Because most white squares sit at the intersection of an Across answer and a Down answer, every letter you place is checked twice. That crossing structure is the whole point of the puzzle: it is what lets you solve a clue you could not have solved cold.

The puzzles here are 10×10 grids built around three themes — Card Games, Casino & Games, and Classic Solitaire — so the answers stay in familiar territory while you learn the solving habits. Click a square to select it, type letters, and use the arrow keys to move around the grid. The Prev and Next buttons move between puzzles, Check Word tells you whether the answer you are sitting on is correct, and Reveal fills it in when you would rather move on than stall. A timer runs at the top if you want to track your pace, and you can ignore it entirely if you would rather not.

How to actually solve one

Beginners usually start at 1 Across and work down the list in order. That is the slowest possible route. Better solvers scan for the clues they are sure of, fill those in wherever they appear, and let the crossing letters do the rest of the work.

  • Take the free ones first. Read every Across clue and every Down clue once, entering only the answers you know instantly. Never leave a certain answer unentered because you are working through the list in order.
  • Let the grid interrogate you. Once a few answers are in, a half-filled slot like S _ A _ E narrows the possibilities enormously. Look at partly filled entries before you look at the clue list again.
  • Use the length. The clue "Poker hand: five cards of same suit" fits FLUSH in five squares and nothing else. Counting squares eliminates plausible-but-wrong answers before you commit ink to them.
  • Match the clue's form to the answer's form. A clue phrased as an infinitive ("To stake money on a card outcome") wants a verb; a clue phrased as a plural noun wants a plural answer. This grammatical agreement is a rule constructors follow, and it is a reliable filter.
  • Mine the theme. When a puzzle is titled "Classic Solitaire", the vocabulary of that world — suits, ranks, piles, deals — is disproportionately likely. A theme is a hint about the whole grid, not decoration.
  • When stuck, doubt an answer rather than the clue. Most dead ends come from one confidently wrong entry poisoning three crossings. If a region refuses to resolve, erase your least certain answer there and try again.

Using Check and Reveal well

Check Word is more useful than it looks. Use it on the answer you feel roughly seventy percent sure about — the one holding up a corner. Confirming or killing that entry unblocks everything crossing it. Using Check on every word as you go turns the puzzle into typing practice; using it never means you can spend twenty minutes building on a wrong letter.

Reveal is for when you have genuinely exhausted a clue. There is no scoring penalty here, and seeing an answer you could not get is how you acquire the vocabulary that makes the next puzzle easier. The words that recur in themed crosswords are a fairly small set, and you learn them mostly by meeting them.

The puzzle runs entirely in your browser with no account and no downloads. If you enjoy this, our strategy guides cover the card games that supply most of the vocabulary in these grids.
